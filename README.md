## What to say

**1 — Title**
"Our topic is: English is a thief. We want to show you where English words really come from."

**2 — 170,000 words**
"English has about 170,000 words in use today. But how many of them are really English? Take a guess."

**3 — Only 1 word in 4**
"Only 25 percent. Three out of four English words were taken from another language."
"But careful — the words we use every day, like *the*, *is*, *man*, *water*, are still the old English ones. English borrowed the big vocabulary, not the basics."

**4 — Where English comes from**
"Before we talk about the stealing: where does English itself come from?"
"It starts north of the Black Sea, in what is now Ukraine and southern Russia, about 6,500 years ago. That language is called Proto-Indo-European. Nobody wrote it down — we worked it out by comparing its children."
"From there it spread across Europe and all the way to India. That is why English and Hindi are distant cousins."
→ point down the staircase, don't read every step
"Only from Old English on do we call it English. And that family line is exactly the 25% from the slide before."

**5 — One sentence, 1000 years**
"This is the same sentence in four periods. Watch the word for *father*."
"*Fæder* — *fadir* — *Father* — *Father*."
"Old English is basically a foreign language. You cannot read it. Same language, 1,000 years."

**6 — The timeline**
"So this is who English stole from. The black box on the left is the original English. Everything else came later."
→ point at 2–3 boxes, don't read all of it

**7 — Why did English borrow so much?**
"Every one of these had a reason."
→ read the five quickly
"Whenever English speakers met new people, English picked up their words. So let's look at how that happened, starting with Latin."

**8 — Latin**
"Latin came twice."
"First around 600, with the Christian missionaries. They taught and wrote in Latin — that's where *school*, *street*, *wine* come from."
"Then again around 1500, when science exploded. Nobody invented words like *data* or *formula* — they just took Latin again."
"Latin is 28% of English. Almost a third. We just stopped noticing."

**9 — The Vikings**
"The Vikings attacked England for 200 years. Then they stopped attacking and just lived there. The two languages mixed."
"*Window* is a Viking word, *vindauga* — wind-eye. A hole for the wind."
"And then this." → *they / them / their*
"These are basic words. Most languages never borrow words like this. English did."

**10 — The French**
"1066. William the Conqueror wins. For 300 years the kings of England speak French, but the farmers keep speaking English."
"So the farmer in the field says *cow*. The rich man at the table says *beef*. Same animal, two words."
"The poor kept the animals, the rich ate them. And 1,000 years later we still use both words."

**11 — Out into the world**
"From 1600, British ships went everywhere. They came home with tea and cotton — and with words."
"*Shampoo* is not English. It's Hindi, and it means to press or to massage."
"This is how English became a world language. Not because it is easy. Because of ships and power."

**12 — Quiz**
"Six words. Where do you think they come from?"
→ let the class shout, then click. Skip some if we are short on time — ketchup and robot are the best.

Answers, in case we present from the PDF and cannot click:
ketchup = Chinese · chocolate = Nahuatl/Mexico · sofa = Arabic ·
robot = Czech · tsunami = Japanese · zero = Arabic

**13 — Korean words**
"And English is still stealing. Right now. From us."
"In September 2021 the Oxford English Dictionary added 26 Korean words in one day."
"A thousand years ago it was the Vikings. Today it is Korea."

**14 — Konglish**
"And it works in the other direction too. Korea makes its own English."
"An American would not understand *hand phone* or *eye shopping*. 50 million Koreans do."
"So is this wrong English? Or is it just Korean English?" → ask, wait

**15 — Ending**
"English is a thief. And that is exactly why it belongs to everybody. Thank you."
