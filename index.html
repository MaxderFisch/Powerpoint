These words don’t fill a gap. They replace German words that worked fine — often just because English sounds more modern.

the case against · 2

Even Germans don’t know how to say it.

Ich habe die Datei   .

“I have ____ the file.” Which one is correct? Vote!

gedownloadetdowngeloadetdownloadet

The Duden dictionary says: downgeloadet. But in real German texts, about one in three people write gedownloadet.

Same problem everywhere: liken → geliked or gelikt? updaten → geupdatet or upgedatet?

the case against · 3

“Come in and find out.”

That was the English slogan of Douglas, a big German perfume shop.

In a 2003 study, many Germans understood it as:
“Come in — and find your way back out.”

Not everyone in Germany speaks English. If people don’t understand it, Denglisch doesn’t bring people together — it shuts them out.

English parts, built into German words and grammar.

And it’s not new: Balkon and Toilette came from French 300 years ago. Today nobody notices.

our answer

Better or worse? Neither. It’s change.

worse when …

  • it replaces a German word for no reason — Meeting instead of Besprechung
  • people don’t understand it — “come in and find out”

better when …

  • it names something new — Computer, Internet, Smartphone
  • it helps Germans talk to the rest of Europe
  • Germans get creative with it — Handy, Beamer

German isn’t dying. It does what it always did: it takes words and makes them German.

your turn

Germany says Handy.
Korea says 핸드폰.

핸드폰 = haen-deu-pon = “hand phone”

Two countries, two made-up English words for the same thing. Neither one is used in the UK or the US.

So — is English making German better or worse?
